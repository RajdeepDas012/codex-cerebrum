### Summary
Mean reversion trading with Bollinger Bands exploits the statistical tendency of prices to return to their moving average after reaching extreme volatility boundaries. It provides a mathematical edge in sideways or ranging markets by identifying overbought and oversold conditions relative to recent price action.

### Key Points
- Bollinger Bands consist of a middle simple moving average (usually 20-period) flanked by upper and lower bands set a specified number of standard deviations (typically 2) away.
- Prices touching or piercing the outer bands indicate statistical extremes, signaling potential reversal points back toward the mean.
- Band width contraction (the 'squeeze') often precedes significant breakouts, which signals a warning to pause mean-reversion strategies.
- Success relies heavily on filtering for sideways or non-trending market regimes using indicators like the Average Directional Index (ADX).

### Code Example
*Calculates Bollinger Bands and generates mean reversion entry signals.*

```python
import pandas as pd

def bollinger_mean_reversion(df, window=20, num_std=2):
    df['sma'] = df['close'].rolling(window=window).mean()
    df['std'] = df['close'].rolling(window=window).std()
    df['upper'] = df['sma'] + (df['std'] * num_std)
    df['lower'] = df['sma'] - (df['std'] * num_std)
    df['signal'] = 0
    df.loc[df['close'] < df['lower'], 'signal'] = 1  # Buy oversold
    df.loc[df['close'] > df['upper'], 'signal'] = -1 # Sell overbought
    return df
```

### Key Points
- VWAP calculates the average price traded throughout the session, weighted by volume at each price level.
- Standard deviation bands (e.g., ±1, ±2 standard deviations) are plotted around VWAP to measure overextended price moves.
- Trades are triggered when the price stretches to the outer deviation bands and then pulls back or reverses toward the central VWAP line.

### Code Example
*Calculates intraday VWAP and standard deviation bands using pandas*

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

def calculate_vwap_bands(df, num_std=2):
    typical_price = (df['high'] + df['low'] + df['close']) / 3
    cumulative_tp_vol = (typical_price * df['volume']).cumsum()
    cumulative_vol = df['volume'].cumsum()
    vwap = cumulative_tp_vol / cumulative_vol
    
    variance = (df['volume'] * (typical_price - vwap) ** 2).cumsum() / cumulative_vol
    stdev = np.sqrt(variance)
    
    df['vwap'] = vwap
    df['upper_band'] = vwap + (num_std * stdev)
    df['lower_band'] = vwap - (num_std * stdev)
    return df
```

### Summary
Low float stock mechanics refer to the extreme price volatility that occurs when a company has a very small number of shares available for public trading. Because supply is severely restricted, even modest shifts in buyer or seller demand can trigger massive, rapid price swings.

### Key Points
- Float represents the total number of shares available to the general public for trading, excluding insider holdings and restricted stock.
- Supply and demand imbalance causes exponential price movement because there are not enough shares to absorb incoming orders smoothly.
- Slippage and wide bid-ask spreads are common due to the lack of market depth and thin order books.
- Short squeezes happen frequently in low float names when trapped short sellers rush to buy limited available shares to cover positions.

### Code Example
*Calculate float percentage from total shares and restricted shares*

```python
def calculate_float(total_shares, insider_shares, institutional_locked):
    float_shares = total_shares - (insider_shares + institutional_locked)
    float_percentage = (float_shares / total_shares) * 100
    return float_shares, float_percentage
```

**❌ Common mistake:**
```python
def calculate_float(total_shares, volume):
    return total_shares - volume
```

### Best Practices
- ✅ Always check the float size before trading high-momentum small-cap stocks.
- ✅ Use strict position sizing and wider stop-losses or limit orders to account for extreme volatility and slippage.
- ✅ Monitor real-time Level 2 order book depth to gauge the actual wall of buyers and sellers.

### Interview Questions
**Q:** Why does a low float stock experience much higher volatility than a high float stock?  
**A:** Because the supply of available shares is tiny, meaning any sudden spike in buy or sell orders cannot be absorbed smoothly, forcing prices to move drastically to find matching liquidity.  

**Q:** What is the primary risk of using market orders on a low float stock?  
**A:** Severe slippage, as the order may execute at significantly worse prices than expected due to thin order books and wide bid-ask spreads.

### Summary
Post-Earnings Announcement Drift (PEAD) is the tendency for a stock's price to continue moving in the direction of an earnings surprise for weeks or months after the report. ### Key Points
- Market participants typically underreact to initial quarterly earnings surprises due to cognitive biases and slow information processing.
- The drift effect can persist anywhere from 30 to 90 days following the initial announcement date.
- Standardized Unexpected Earnings (SUE) is the quantitative metric most commonly used to measure the magnitude of the surprise relative to historical volatility.
- High institutional ownership and strong analyst estimate revisions often amplify the persistence and magnitude of the drift.


### Best Practices
- ✅ Combine SUE calculations with concurrent upward or downward revisions in forward analyst guidance.
- ✅ Filter for companies with sufficient average daily volume to ensure smooth entry and exit during the drift period.
- ✅ Scale into positions gradually rather than executing a single large market order immediately after the open.

### Interview Questions
**Q:** What is PEAD and why does it contradict the Efficient Market Hypothesis?  
**A:** PEAD is the tendency for stock prices to drift in the direction of an earnings surprise for weeks. It contradicts the strong or semi-strong forms of the EMH because stock prices fail to instantly incorporate all publicly available earnings information.  

**Q:** How do you calculate Standardized Unexpected Earnings (SUE) and why is it important?  
**A:** SUE is calculated as actual earnings minus consensus estimates, divided by the standard deviation of forecast errors over a prior period. It normalizes the surprise, allowing comparisons across different stocks regardless of their historical volatility.
