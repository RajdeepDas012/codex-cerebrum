**Category:** `trading-strategies`  
**Difficulty:** `advanced`  
**Language:** `language-agnostic`  

### Summary
Vanna measures the sensitivity of option delta to changes in implied volatility, while charm measures the sensitivity of option delta to the passage of time (delta decay). Understanding these second-order Greeks allows advanced traders to anticipate subtle, non-linear shifts in market maker hedging flows before they become obvious in first-order Greeks.

### Key Points
- Vanna links spot price movements with changes in volatility, driving accelerated dealer re-hedging when IV spikes during market selloffs.
- Charm (or delta decay) dictates how option delta drifts systematically as expiration approaches, particularly forcing steady flows near OpEx.
- Positive vanna in out-of-the-money calls means rising volatility increases their delta, forcing dealers to buy underlying stock.
- Charm effects intensify drastically during the final week of options expiration, creating predictable intraday directional biases.

### Code Example
*Calculates approximate vanna and charm for a European option using Black-Scholes formulas.*

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

def calculate_vanna_charm(S, K, T, r, sigma):
    d1 = (np.log(S / K) + (r + 0.5 * sigma**2) * T) / (sigma * np.sqrt(T))
    d2 = d1 - sigma * np.sqrt(T)
    vanna = -np.exp(-d1**2 / 2) * d2 / (sqrt_2_pi := np.sqrt(2 * np.pi) * sigma * S) * S # simplified
    charm = -np.exp(-d1**2 / 2) * (2 * r * T - d2 * sigma * np.sqrt(T)) / (2 * T * sigma * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))
    return vanna, charm
```

### Summary
Implied Volatility Rank (IVR) measures where a stock's current implied volatility sits relative to its high and low extremes over a specific historical lookback period, typically one year. It matters because raw IV numbers cannot be compared across different stocks, whereas IVR normalizes the value on a 0 to 100 scale to help options traders identify expensive or cheap premium.

### Key Points
- Calculated as (Current IV - 52-week Low IV) / (52-week High IV - 52-week Low IV) * 100.
- Normalizes volatility so a high-priced stock with 40% IV can be accurately compared to a low-priced stock with 20% IV.
- An IVR of 100 means current implied volatility is at its highest point over the past year.
- An IVR of 0 means current implied volatility is at its lowest point over the past year.

### Code Example
*Calculates Implied Volatility Rank given current IV and a historical array of IV values*

```python
def calculate_ivr(current_iv, historical_ivs):
    iv_low = min(historical_ivs)
    iv_high = max(historical_ivs)
    if iv_high == iv_low:
        return 0.0
    return ((current_iv - iv_low) / (iv_high - iv_low)) * 100
```

**❌ Common mistake:**

- ✅ Pair IVR readings with upcoming catalyst schedules like earnings announcements to spot mispriced options.
- ✅ Combine IVR with directional technical analysis to select appropriate credit or debit strategies.

*Calculates Implied Volatility Percentile from a pandas Series of historical IV values.*

import pandas as pd

def calculate_ivp(iv_series, lookback=252):
    recent_iv = iv_series.iloc[-lookback:]

## Run #436 — 04:32 UTC

**Topic:** Vertical spread risk reward — debit vs credit spread comparison  
**Category:** `trading-strategies`  
**Difficulty:** `intermediate`  
**Language:** `python`  

### Summary
A vertical spread involves buying and selling options of the same type and expiration but at different strikes, creating a direct trade-off between win rate and payout. Debit spreads offer defined risk with asymmetric upside potential, whereas credit spreads collect immediate premium with a higher statistical probability of expiring out-of-the-money.

### Key Points
- Debit spreads (bull call or bear put) are net cash outflows, meaning maximum loss is strictly limited to the upfront premium paid.
- Credit spreads (bull put or bear call) are net cash inflows, meaning maximum profit is capped at the initial credit received.
- Credit spreads generally offer higher probabilities of profit (PoP) because they win if the underlying stays still, moves in favor, or moves slightly against the position.
- Risk-to-reward ratios are inverted: debit spreads risk a small amount to make a larger amount, while credit spreads risk a larger amount to collect a smaller upfront credit.

### Code Example
*Calculates max profit, max loss, and risk-reward ratio for both debit and credit vertical spreads.*

```python
def analyze_vertical_spread(long_strike, short_strike, net_price, is_debit):
    width = abs(long_strike - short_strike)
    if is_debit:
        max_loss = net_price
        max_profit = width - net_price
    else:
        max_credit = net_price
        max_loss = width - net_price
        max_profit = max_credit
    return max_profit, max_loss
```

**❌ Common mistake:**
```python
def analyze_vertical_spread(long_strike, short_strike, net_price, is_debit):
    # Mistake: treating credit spread risk as just the net price instead of width minus credit
    return width - net_price, net_price
```

### Best Practices
- ✅ Select debit spreads when you have strong directional conviction and expect an expansion in implied volatility.
- ✅ Deploy credit spreads in flat or neutral-to-mildly directional environments where you want time decay (theta) to work in your favor.
- ✅ Target a minimum risk-to-reward ratio of 1:1 for debit spreads and ensure credit spreads collect at least one-third of the total strike width.

### Interview Questions
**Q:** What is the structural difference in profit and loss profiles between a debit spread and a credit spread?  
**A:** A debit spread costs money upfront, offering limited risk and higher percentage upside if the market moves in your direction. A credit spread generates cash upfront, offering limited reward and higher risk of loss if the market makes a sharp adverse move.  

**Q:** Why might a trader choose a credit spread over a debit spread despite having a less favorable risk-to-reward ratio?  
**A:** Credit spreads have a higher statistical probability of profit (PoP) because they profit in multiple scenarios: if the underlying goes up, stays flat, or drops only slightly, allowing the trader to win more frequently.

**Topic:** Straddle breakeven calculation — expected move from options pricing  
**Category:** `trading-strategies`  
**Difficulty:** `intermediate`  
**Language:** `python`  

### Summary
A straddle breakeven calculation determines the upper and lower stock price thresholds required for a long straddle to cover the total premium paid. Traders use this combined call and put option pricing to quickly estimate the market's expected move for a specific expiration date.

### Key Points
- The total cost (or net debit) of a long straddle is the sum of the call premium and the put premium paid.
- The upper breakeven point is calculated by adding the total premium paid to the strike price.
- The lower breakeven point is calculated by subtracting the total premium paid from the strike price.
- Market makers use the ATM straddle price roughly as a 1 standard deviation expected move approximation for the underlying asset over the given timeframe.

### Code Example
*Calculates the upper and lower breakeven points and the market expected move for a long straddle.*

```python
def calculate_straddle_metrics(strike, call_price, put_price):
    total_premium = call_price + put_price
    upper_breakeven = strike + total_premium
    lower_breakeven = strike - total_premium
    expected_move_pct = (total_premium / strike) * 100
    return upper_breakeven, lower_breakeven, expected_move_pct
```

**❌ Common mistake:**
```python

    # Mistake: forgetting that you must pay for both the call and the put
    upper_breakeven = strike + call_price
    lower_breakeven = strike - put_price
    return upper_breakeven, lower_breakeven
```

### Best Practices
- ✅ Always include transaction fees and slippage when calculating exact breakeven points for high-frequency or retail execution.
- ✅ Use the straddle expected move as a baseline filter before entering directional spreads or earnings plays.
- ✅ Remember that options prices imply expected moves through expiration, not necessarily intraday peaks.

### Interview Questions
**Q:** How do you quickly estimate the expected move of an underlying asset using an at-the-money (ATM) straddle?  
**A:** You add the price of the ATM call and the ATM put together (the straddle price); this combined premium represents the market's expected +/- move from the strike price by expiration.  

**Q:** If a stock is trading at $100, and the 100-strike ATM straddle costs $6.00, what are the breakeven points for a long straddle buyer?  
**A:** The upper breakeven is $106.00 ($100 strike + $6.00 premium) and the lower breakeven is $94.00 ($100 strike - $6.00 premium).
